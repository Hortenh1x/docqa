# Giving Feedback

## 1. Purpose

Feedback is how we grow as individuals and as a company. At Kranich Software, we believe that honest, timely, and kind feedback helps us build better products, stronger teams, and a more supportive workplace. This document explains how we give and receive feedback at Kranich, what you can expect from others, and where to turn if you need support.

Feedback here is not a once-a-year ritual. It is a daily practice woven into how we collaborate, review our work, and treat one another. Whether you are praising a colleague for a great presentation or gently flagging a missed deadline, your words matter. This policy helps ensure that feedback lands well and leads to positive change.

## 2. Who this is for

This document is for everyone at Kranich Software — all employees, in every role, in every location. Whether you work from the Ritterstraße office in Berlin, the Rua do Alecrim office in Lisbon, or remotely from your home in Poland, Spain, France, or the United Kingdom, the same principles apply.

Feedback flows in all directions. It is not just managers giving feedback to their reports. Engineers give feedback to product managers. Customer success managers give feedback to the Routing Core team. New joiners give feedback to veterans. And leaders actively seek feedback from everyone on their teams.

If you manage people, you have an additional responsibility: to create an environment where feedback feels safe and where your team members know that their input is valued. If you are new to leadership, your own manager and the People & Culture team can support you in developing this skill.

## 3. Process

We follow a simple, human process for giving feedback. It can be summarized in three steps: prepare, deliver, and follow up.

**Prepare.** Before you give feedback, take a moment to reflect. What specifically happened? What was the impact on you, the team, or the work? What would you like to see change or continue? Being specific helps the other person understand exactly what you mean. Vague comments like "you should be more proactive" are hard to act on. Instead, describe the situation and its effect in concrete terms.

**Deliver.** Choose the right moment and the right channel. For positive feedback, share it openly — in a team channel, in a meeting, or in a quick message. For constructive feedback, prefer a private conversation. If you are remote, a video call is usually better than a chat message, because tone and body language are easier to read. Speak from your own perspective, using "I" statements. For example: "I noticed that the deployment was delayed, and I felt concerned because we had promised the client a date."

**Follow up.** Feedback is not a one-off event. After a constructive conversation, check in with the person after a few days to see how things are going. Offer your help if they are working on a change. If you received feedback, let the giver know what you plan to do differently, and thank them for their honesty.

When giving feedback, aim for a balance. If you only ever point out problems, your words lose weight. If you only ever praise, people miss opportunities to improve. Both are part of the same practice.

Example: Anna, a backend engineer in Lisbon, notices that her colleague Marek has been consistently thorough in documenting his API changes. She sends him a short message in the team channel praising his clarity, and mentions it in her next one-on-one with her manager, Ines Duarte. Later, when Marek misses a code review deadline, Anna schedules a private call with him. She describes the impact — the release was blocked — and asks if there is anything she can do to help. Marek appreciates the directness and explains that he was juggling several client visits in Poland. They agree on a simple way to communicate about upcoming deadlines.

## 4. Timelines

Feedback should be timely. The closer it is to the event, the more relevant it feels. Do not save up observations for months and then release them all at once. If something happens that warrants feedback, share it promptly — within a few days, while the memory is fresh.

That said, some moments deserve a pause. If you are feeling angry or frustrated, wait until you have calmed down before speaking. A short delay is better than a harsh word you cannot take back.

There are also structured moments for feedback built into our rhythm. Your manager will schedule regular one-on-one conversations with you, and these are good places to discuss progress, challenges, and development. Additionally, we run a lightweight peer feedback process a couple of times a year, coordinated by People & Culture. You will receive an invitation through Fern with clear instructions when it is time to participate.

If you are going on leave or changing teams, take the opportunity to give feedback to your colleagues before you go. A departing perspective can be especially valuable.

## 5. Questions

If you are unsure about how to give feedback in a particular situation, or if you received feedback that felt unfair or hurtful, you have options.

- **Your manager.** Start here. They know you and your work best and can help you interpret feedback or prepare for a difficult conversation.
- **People & Culture.** Lea and her colleagues are available to coach you on how to phrase feedback, or to mediate if a conversation did not go well. Reach out via people@kranich.example.
- **Security and confidentiality.** If the feedback relates to a potential breach of our policies or something that makes you uncomfortable, you can always contact security@kranich.example or follow the reporting paths described in the relevant policy documents, such as POL-004. Your confidentiality will be respected.
- **Grove.** Our intranet contains additional resources, including templates for feedback conversations and recordings of past workshops. Browse the People & Culture section to learn more.

Remember that receiving feedback is a skill too. When someone takes the time to give you feedback, listen fully before responding. Ask clarifying questions if needed. You do not have to agree with everything, but you should try to understand the other person's perspective. Thank them for their courage.

Feedback is a gift. At Kranich, we give it generously and receive it graciously. Together, we make each other better.
