# Security Incident Policy

## 1. Purpose

This policy explains how we handle security incidents at Kranich Software GmbH. It describes what counts as an incident, how to report one, and how we contain and communicate about it. We want everyone to feel confident raising concerns early, without fear of blame. Acting quickly and transparently protects our customers, our people, and our reputation.

This version supersedes version 1.0 of this policy. It reflects how our teams have matured since then, and it clarifies roles and expectations. The Information Security team owns this policy, but every employee plays a part in keeping Kranich Route Cloud secure.

## 2. What counts as an incident

A security incident is any event that could compromise the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of our systems, data, or infrastructure. It does not matter whether the event was intentional or accidental. If you are unsure, treat it as an incident and report it.

Examples include:

- Unauthorized access to a customer account, internal system, or repository.
- Loss or theft of a company laptop, phone, or other device that may contain work data.
- Suspected phishing emails that tricked someone into entering credentials.
- Malware detected on a workstation or server.
- Accidental exposure of sensitive data, such as sending customer information to the wrong recipient.
- Unusual system behavior that might indicate an intrusion.
- A misconfigured service that allows public access to private data.

Example: Anna, a backend engineer in Lisbon, notices that a log file contains entries from an IP address she does not recognize. Even though nothing seems broken, she reports it promptly. The investigation shows it was a misconfigured monitoring agent. Reporting early meant the team corrected it before any data left our environment.

Example: Deniz, a customer success manager in Berlin, realizes she sent a spreadsheet with customer names to the wrong external contact. She reports it immediately. The incident response team contains the exposure and assesses whether any notification is needed.

## 3. Reporting

If you suspect a security incident, report it as soon as you become aware. Suspected incidents are reported within 24 hours to security@kranich.example. This includes incidents you observe outside of your regular working hours. If you are unsure whether something qualifies, report it anyway.

Do not wait for confirmation before reporting. Do not attempt to investigate deeply on your own. Your first step is always to notify the Information Security team. Include as much detail as you have: what happened, when you noticed it, which systems or data were involved, and any steps you already took.

If the incident involves a lost or stolen device, follow the runbook SEC-RB-LOSTDEVICE in addition to emailing security@kranich.example. That runbook contains specific steps for device-related incidents. Please do not wait to see whether the device turns up before reporting.

For incidents that involve personal data of employees, the Information Security team will coordinate with People & Culture as needed. You do not need to contact people@kranich.example yourself unless the incident is about a leave request or personal data visible in Fern.

Example: Marek, a field solutions engineer working with clients in Poland, realizes his laptop bag was stolen from a rental car. He emails security@kranich.example immediately and then follows the steps in SEC-RB-LOSTDEVICE. He does not try to remotely wipe the device himself, because the runbook explains who handles that.

## 4. Containment

Once the Information Security team acknowledges your report, they will take ownership of the response. Containment aims to limit the damage and prevent the incident from spreading. The team may ask you to take specific actions, such as disconnecting a device from the network, changing passwords, or disabling an account.

Follow the instructions from the Information Security team promptly. Do not take containment steps on your own unless the team explicitly asks you to. Well-intentioned actions can sometimes destroy evidence or make the situation worse.

The incident response team may involve other colleagues as needed, such as the engineering manager for the affected system or the Head of Workplace & Operations for physical security matters. The team will keep you informed about what they need from you and when.

Containment is not the same as resolution. A contained incident may still require deeper investigation, cleanup, or recovery work. The team will manage that work and communicate about progress.

Example: Sofia, a product manager in Fleet Insights working remotely from Spain, receives an alert that her account was used to log in from an unfamiliar location. She reports it. The Information Security team asks her to change her password and enable multi-factor authentication before continuing work. She does so right away. Later, the team confirms the login attempt was blocked and no data was accessed.

## 5. Disclosure decisions

Access: Leadership only

The decision about whether to disclose an incident to customers, partners, or the public rests with the Leadership team. The CEO, CTO, CFO, and the Head of Information Security will review each incident on a case-by-case basis. They consider legal obligations, contractual commitments, customer impact, and our values of transparency.

Leadership decides on customer disclosure within 48 hours of confirmation. Confirmation means the Information Security team has verified that an incident occurred and has a reasonable understanding of its scope. If Leadership needs more time to assess a complex situation, they will document the reason and revisit the decision promptly.

The Information Security team prepares a summary for Leadership that includes what happened, which data was affected, what we have contained, and what we recommend. Leadership may consult with Customer Success or Go-to-Market before deciding, especially if the incident affects a specific client relationship.

If Leadership decides to disclose, the Customer Success team coordinates the communication with affected customers. The tone is factual, honest, and helpful. We explain what we know, what we are doing, and what customers can do to protect themselves. We do not speculate about causes or impacts we have not confirmed.

Employees who are not part of Leadership or the designated response team should not share details about an ongoing incident with customers or externally. If a customer asks you about a suspected incident, refer them to your manager or the Customer Success team. If you are unsure whether information is public, check with the Information Security team before speaking.

Example: A security incident affects a subset of customer accounts. The Information Security team confirms the scope and briefs Leadership within the required window. Leadership decides to inform the affected customers through their named Customer Success manager. Deniz receives a prepared summary and contacts her assigned accounts. She does not share technical details beyond what the summary provides.

## 6. Questions

If you have questions about this policy, how to report an incident, or what counts as an incident, contact the Information Security team at security@kranich.example. For questions about how this policy relates to employee data or workplace matters, contact people@kranich.example.

You can also find related guidance on Grove, our intranet. The runbook SEC-RB-LOSTDEVICE is available there, as are contact details for the Information Security team. If you are unsure about anything, asking is always the right choice. We would rather answer many questions than receive one report too late.
