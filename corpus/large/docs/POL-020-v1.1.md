# Meeting Culture Guideline

## 1. Purpose

Meetings are a means to an end: they help us align, decide, and move work forward. They are not the work itself. This guideline describes how we plan, run, and attend meetings at Kranich Software GmbH so that everyone’s time is respected, regardless of where they sit or which team they belong to.

This version supersedes version 1.0 of POL-020. It reflects what we have learned since the company grew beyond a single office and became remote-first across Germany, Portugal, Spain, Poland, France, and the United Kingdom. The Leadership Team owns this policy, and we review it whenever our ways of working change materially.

We wrote this guideline for everyone at Kranich — engineers, product managers, customer success managers, field solutions engineers, and people in supporting functions. It applies to internal meetings and to meetings with external parties where Kranich hosts the call. If you are unsure whether a meeting is necessary, err on the side of not scheduling one and communicate asynchronously instead.

## 2. Focus time

Deep work requires uninterrupted stretches of time. We protect that time deliberately. Every weekday afternoon holds 2 hours of company-wide focus time with no meetings. During those hours, you can decline any meeting invitation without explanation, and you should not expect an immediate reply to messages.

Focus time is not a break. It is a shared commitment to let each other think. If you are in the Ritterstraße office in Berlin or the Rua do Alecrim office in Lisbon, treat focus time as quiet time in shared spaces. If you work from home, let your household know that a call is unlikely.

Example: Anna, a backend engineer in Routing Core in Lisbon, uses focus time to reason through a complex routing constraint. She knows her colleagues in Berlin will not schedule a call during that window, so she can safely block the time in her calendar as busy.

If you need additional focus beyond the company-wide window, you are welcome to mark your calendar as busy. The default expectation is that you will be available for meetings outside the protected window, but occasional blocks for deep work are normal and healthy.

## 3. Running a good meeting

Every meeting should have a clear purpose and a named facilitator. The facilitator is responsible for the agenda, the pace, and the outcome. If a meeting lacks an agenda, the facilitator should cancel it or send one promptly.

Decision meetings are capped at 6 people. This limit keeps discussions sharp and ensures that everyone present can genuinely contribute. If a decision affects a wider group, the facilitator shares the outcome afterward and invites feedback within a reasonable window rather than expanding the room.

Before you schedule a meeting, ask yourself: what do we need to achieve, and who truly needs to be in the room? Invite people because they can contribute to the outcome, not because they might be interested. You can always share notes with people who are not invited.

For recurring meetings, review the attendee list and the agenda at least once per quarter. Cancel sessions that no longer serve a purpose. It is better to end a recurring meeting early than to let it drift.

Example: Deniz, a customer success manager in Berlin, wants to discuss a client escalation with Marek, a field solutions engineer in Poland, and Sofia, a product manager in Fleet Insights in Spain. Deniz keeps the meeting to the three of them plus herself, because the decision does not require anyone else. Afterward, she posts a short summary in Grove for the wider Customer Success team.

Meeting hygiene matters. Start on time, end on time, and allocate a minute or two at the end to confirm next steps and owners. If you are running late or must leave early, say so at the start. If you are attending remotely, join from a quiet space and mute yourself when you are not speaking.

## 4. Async first

Asynchronous communication is our default. We work across time zones from Berlin to Lisbon and beyond, and we do not assume that everyone can attend a live call at a moment’s notice. Write things down, share them in Grove or the appropriate channel, and let people respond when they are able.

Use a meeting only when a live conversation adds value: brainstorming that benefits from rapid back-and-forth, resolving a conflict, or making a decision that requires discussion rather than a simple vote. For everything else, prefer documents, recorded updates, or written questions.

When you share a document for feedback, state the deadline for comments clearly and name the decision owner. If you need an answer, ask a direct question rather than posting a document and hoping for replies.

Example: Sofia, a product manager in Fleet Insights working remotely from Spain, wants input on a proposed change to how we present fleet health scores. Instead of scheduling a call with several stakeholders, she writes a short proposal, posts it in the Fleet Insights channel, and asks for comments by the end of the week. Colleagues in Poland and the United Kingdom respond asynchronously, and Sofia incorporates their feedback before the next team sync.

If you schedule a meeting, share the agenda and any pre-read material at least a day in advance. Do not use meeting time for silent reading. If attendees have not had a chance to read the material, consider rescheduling or summarizing the key points verbally and deferring the decision.

## 5. Questions

If you have questions about this guideline, start with your manager or your team lead. For practical questions about scheduling tools or calendar settings, contact it-help@kranich.example. For questions about how this policy interacts with other policies, such as POL-004 on remote work, reach out to people@kranich.example and we will point you to the right owner.

If you believe a meeting you attend is unnecessary, wasteful, or poorly facilitated, say so constructively — to the facilitator or to your manager. We would rather hear about a problem than let it persist. Similarly, if you notice that a recurring meeting has drifted from its purpose, propose canceling it or changing its format.

The Leadership Team reviews this guideline periodically. We welcome suggestions for improvement from anyone at Kranich, whether you work from the Berlin office, the Lisbon office, or remotely from another country. Send your ideas to people@kranich.example, and we will consider them in the next review cycle.

Example: Lea, a people operations specialist in Berlin, receives a question from a new colleague about whether they may schedule a meeting during focus time for an urgent client call. Lea explains the rule, then offers an alternative: the colleague can ask the client to move the call, or if that is impossible, they can request an exception from their manager. The guideline is firm, but it is not rigid — we trust people to use judgment in rare situations.

Meetings are a tool for collaboration, not a substitute for it. By protecting focus time, keeping decision rooms small, and defaulting to asynchronous work, we give each other the space to do our best work. Thank you for helping us keep meetings meaningful.
