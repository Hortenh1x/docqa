# Working Time & Overtime Policy

*Superseded by version 2.0.*

## 1. Purpose

This policy explains how we think about working time at Kranich Software GmbH. We want everyone to do meaningful work without burning out, and we know that clear expectations help with that. The policy applies to all employees in Germany, Portugal, Spain, Poland, France and the United Kingdom, regardless of whether you usually work from home, from one of our offices in Berlin or Lisbon, or at a client site.

We are a remote-first company, and we trust you to manage your day. At the same time, we have legal and operational obligations to keep working time within reasonable limits, and we have practical rules so that overtime stays the exception and gets compensated fairly.

This policy is owned by People & Culture. If a rule in this document conflicts with a local law or a collective agreement that applies to you, the local law or agreement wins. When in doubt, ask your manager or reach out to people@kranich.example.

## 2. Working time

Working time is the time you spend performing your duties for Kranich. It includes your regular work, client calls, internal meetings, and time spent on business travel during what would otherwise be your working hours. It does not include your lunch break, other unpaid breaks, or your commute between home and the office.

Our standard working week runs from Monday to Friday. We do not prescribe fixed start and end times; you and your manager agree on a schedule that fits your team's collaboration needs and your personal circumstances. What matters is that you are available for the meetings and tasks your role requires, and that you coordinate with colleagues across time zones.

Working time may not exceed 44 hours in any week. This limit includes overtime. We count a week from Monday to Sunday. If you work in a jurisdiction with a stricter weekly limit, the stricter limit applies to you.

We expect you to take breaks during the day. A break of at least thirty minutes is recommended when you work more than six hours. You should also take time away from the screen regularly; short walks or stretching count as breaks only if they are not part of your duties.

Example: Anna, a backend engineer in Lisbon, usually starts at nine and finishes around five-thirty. On a week with a late deployment, she works until seven on two evenings. She keeps an eye on her total so that she stays under the weekly limit.

If you regularly need more than the standard hours to complete your work, speak with your manager. That is usually a sign that priorities need adjusting or that the team needs more support, not that you should work longer. We do not expect anyone to answer messages outside their agreed working hours, unless they are on call or have explicitly agreed otherwise with their team.

## 3. Overtime and compensation

Overtime is any time you work beyond your individually agreed weekly hours, up to the weekly limit in §2. Overtime is not routine. It happens when there is a genuine spike in workload, a critical incident, a client deadline, or another exceptional situation. Your manager should know about overtime before or shortly after it occurs.

You should not work overtime just because a colleague does, and you should not feel pressure to work longer to appear committed. If you feel that expectation, tell your manager or contact People & Culture. We evaluate performance on outcomes, not on hours.

Overtime is compensated with time off within 16 weeks. This means that when you work extra hours, you should take an equivalent amount of time off during the following sixteen weeks, at a time that suits you and your team. Time off in lieu is the primary form of compensation for overtime across Kranich, and it applies to all employees covered by this policy.

You do not need to submit a formal request for overtime compensation in advance. Instead, agree with your manager on a day or half-day to take off, and record it in Fern as you would any other time off. If you have accumulated overtime but have not been able to take the time off within the sixteen-week window, talk to your manager. Together you will find a way to clear the balance promptly, for example by scheduling the time off before the window closes.

Example: Deniz, a customer success manager in Berlin, works an extra two hours one evening to support a client during a critical migration. The following week, she leaves early on a Thursday afternoon and records the two hours as time off in Fern. Her manager approves it, and her overtime balance is cleared.

Overtime during on-call duty is handled separately. If you are on call, you are compensated for being reachable, and any actual work you perform while on call counts as working time and is subject to the same time-off compensation described above. Your team lead can explain the on-call arrangements that apply to your role.

We do not pay overtime as additional salary. The time-off model keeps things simple and fair, and it protects your rest. If local law requires a different approach, we follow that law.

## 4. Time tracking

We track working time so that we can protect the limits in this policy and so that you can see your own balance. You are responsible for recording your working time accurately in Fern. You should record your regular hours and any overtime in the same place, ideally at the end of each working day or at the latest by the end of the week.

Recording time is not about surveillance. It is a tool for your own planning and for compliance with the legal limits that apply in the countries where we operate. Your manager can see your recorded time, and People & Culture has access for policy administration. The data is handled confidentially in line with our privacy principles.

If you work a standard week with no overtime, a simple weekly entry that reflects your agreed hours is enough. If your hours vary, record them as they actually occur. If you travel for work, record travel time that falls within working time as working time.

Example: Marek, a field solutions engineer who works with clients in Poland, spends a day travelling to a client site. He records the travel during business hours as working time and his overnight stay as a break. He does not record the evening dinner with the client, because that is not working time.

If you notice that your recorded hours regularly exceed your agreed hours, raise it with your manager. If you see that a colleague's recorded hours look high, you do not need to police them; People & Culture reviews patterns and will reach out if something looks off.

Occasionally, system issues may prevent you from recording time. In that case, note your hours somewhere safe and enter them into Fern as soon as the system is available again. If you need help with Fern, contact it-help@kranich.example. For questions about what counts as working time, contact people@kranich.example.

## 5. Questions

If you have questions about this policy, start with your manager. They can help you apply it to your day-to-day work. For questions about time recording in Fern, contact it-help@kranich.example. For anything related to your employment contract, local rules, or how this policy interacts with other policies such as POL-004, contact People & Culture at people@kranich.example.

We want this policy to feel straightforward. If a situation is not covered here, use your judgement, talk to your manager, and err on the side of rest. Working long hours does not help you, your team, or our clients. Taking your time off and staying within the weekly limit is part of doing your job well.
