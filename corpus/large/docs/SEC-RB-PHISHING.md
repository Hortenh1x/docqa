# Phishing Runbook

## 1. Purpose

Phishing is one of the most common ways attackers try to reach Kranich employees. A single careless click can put customer data, internal systems, or your personal credentials at risk. This runbook helps you recognize suspicious messages, react calmly, and report them the right way.

It applies to all email, chat messages, calendar invitations, and documents shared with you — on any device, including your work phone and personal devices used for work.

## 2. Spotting phishing

Phishing messages try to make you act quickly or quietly. They often play on urgency, fear, curiosity, or helpfulness. Trust your instincts: if something feels off, slow down.

Look for these common signs:

- The sender address looks familiar but is slightly wrong, for example a misspelled domain or an extra character.
- The message asks you to click a link, open an attachment, or enter your password.
- The tone is urgent: "your account will be closed," "confirm immediately," or "your manager requested this."
- The greeting is generic, such as "Dear colleague," instead of using your name.
- The message contains odd grammar, unusual phrasing, or a style that does not match how the sender normally writes.
- A link's visible text does not match the real destination. Hover over it to preview the address.
- You receive an unexpected attachment, especially one ending in `.zip`, `.docm`, or `.html`.
- A message arrives from a known contact but is out of character, for example a colleague suddenly asking you to buy gift cards or review a strange document.

Phishing can arrive through other channels too. A chat message from someone you do not know, a calendar invite with a suspicious link, or a fake login page that looks like Grove or Fern are all possibilities.

Example: Anna, a backend engineer in Lisbon, receives an email that appears to come from the CTO, Jonas Weber. The address shows `jonas.weber@kranich.example` but on closer inspection the domain is `kranich-example.com`. The message asks her to review an attached "expense overview" within a few hours. Anna notices the odd domain and the unusual attachment, and she does not open it.

## 3. What to do

Follow these steps whenever you suspect a phishing attempt.

**Do not click, download, or reply.** Do not forward the message to anyone else. Do not delete it either — you may need it for the investigation.

**Report it promptly.** Use the phishing button in your email client if available, or forward the message as an attachment to security@kranich.example. If the message arrived in chat, take a screenshot and send it to the same address. If you are unsure whether something is phishing, report it anyway. There is no penalty for a false alarm.

**Mark the message as phishing** in your email client, if that option exists. This helps the security team improve automatic filters.

**Change your password** if you already clicked a link or entered credentials. Use 1Password to generate a new, strong password for the affected account. Then report what happened to security@kranich.example right away.

**Disconnect from the network** if you opened an attachment and your device behaves strangely — for example, files encrypting, unexpected pop-ups, or slow performance. Unplug the network cable or disable Wi-Fi, then contact security@kranich.example from another device.

**Do not confront the sender.** If a colleague's account is compromised, the attacker may be reading their messages. Let the security team handle communication with the affected person.

Example: Deniz, a customer success manager in Berlin, receives a calendar invitation from an external address claiming to be a client. The invitation contains a link to a document-sharing site Deniz has never used. Deniz reports the invitation to security@kranich.example without clicking, and the security team confirms it was a phishing attempt targeting customer-facing staff.

**After you report, you are done.** The security team will investigate. You do not need to warn your team, post in chat, or take further action — that can cause confusion. If the team needs more information, they will contact you.

## 4. Questions

If you have questions about phishing or any security concern, reach out to security@kranich.example. The team is happy to help you review a suspicious message, even if you are not sure it is phishing.

For general questions about security awareness training, see the relevant policy in the Grove policy library, or speak with your manager. Priya Nayar, Head of Information Security, and her team regularly share updates about new phishing trends on Grove.

You can also consult POL-004 for the broader information security framework, and FIN-RATES-2026 for guidelines on reporting suspicious financial communications. Neither replaces this runbook — this is your day-to-day guide for what to do in the moment.

Remember: a few extra seconds of attention can prevent a serious incident. When in doubt, report. The security team would rather review a hundred harmless messages than miss one real attack.
