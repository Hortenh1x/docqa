# Meeting Culture Guideline

## 1. Purpose

Meetings are a means to an end: they help us align, decide, and move work forward. At Kranich Software GmbH, we also know that meetings cost us something precious — the uninterrupted time we need to build great software and support our customers. This guideline sets out how we plan, run, and question meetings so that they serve us rather than the other way around.

This policy applies to everyone at Kranich, whether you work from the Ritterstraße office in Berlin, the Rua do Alecrim office in Lisbon, or remotely from your home in Germany, Portugal, Spain, Poland, France, or the United Kingdom. It covers internal meetings as well as meetings with customers and partners, though we always apply good judgment when an external party expects a more formal rhythm.

We believe that a healthy meeting culture is a shared responsibility. Meeting hosts own the agenda and the outcome. Attendees own their presence and their contribution. And every one of us owns the courage to decline an invitation that does not need us.

## 2. Focus time

Deep work is the foundation of our craft. Writing code, analysing data, designing a solution, or preparing a customer workshop all require stretches of uninterrupted concentration. Constant context switching fragments our thinking and lowers the quality of everything we do.

To protect this, every weekday afternoon holds 2 hours of company-wide focus time with no meetings. During this window, you will not receive meeting invitations, and we ask you not to schedule any. This applies to all teams and all locations, including customer-facing roles. If a customer requests a meeting during focus time, offer an alternative slot the same day or the next working day; in urgent situations, speak with your manager first.

Focus time is not a break — it is working time, and you are still expected to be reachable for urgent matters through your usual channels. If something genuinely cannot wait, send a direct message rather than scheduling a meeting. We trust you to use your judgment about what counts as urgent.

Example: Anna, a backend engineer in Lisbon, uses focus time to work through a complex routing algorithm. Her colleague in Berlin knows not to invite her to a sync during that window and instead leaves a thoughtful comment on the pull request. Anna picks it up when her focus block ends.

## 3. Running a good meeting

A good meeting starts before it begins. Every meeting must have a clear purpose and an agenda shared in advance, even if the agenda is just a few bullet points. If you cannot articulate why the meeting exists, it probably should not. If you are invited to a meeting without an agenda, ask the host for one — or ask whether the meeting can happen without you.

Keep the attendee list small. Invite people who need to contribute or decide, and include others only if they will benefit from hearing the discussion. You can always share notes afterward with people who were not in the room. As a rule of thumb, if you find yourself wondering why someone is on the invitation, have the conversation about removing them — kindly.

Start on time and end on time. Late starts punish punctual people, and overruns bleed into everyone else's day. If you finish early, end early. There is no virtue in filling the remaining minutes.

Assign a note-taker before the meeting starts. Notes should capture decisions, action items, and owners — not a transcript. At the end of the meeting, take a moment to recap decisions and confirm who does what next. If no action items or decisions emerged, consider whether the meeting needed to happen at all.

For recurring meetings, review the series every few months. Ask yourselves: is this still serving us? Would an async update or a shorter cadence work? Cancelling a recurring meeting is a sign of health, not failure.

Example: Deniz, a customer success manager in Berlin, hosts a weekly check-in with a client. She notices that most weeks, the client has nothing new to share and the meeting runs on pleasantries. She proposes moving to a monthly call with a written update in between. The client agrees, and Deniz uses the reclaimed time to prepare a deeper quarterly review.

## 4. Async first

We are a remote-first company, and our default mode of work is asynchronous. Before you schedule a meeting, ask yourself whether the goal can be achieved without one. A document, a recorded demo, a shared spreadsheet, or a thoughtful message on a collaboration channel often works better than a live call — especially when participants span time zones from Lisbon to Warsaw.

When you do work asynchronously, be generous with context. Write clear subject lines, summarise decisions in a few sentences, and state explicitly when you need a response and by when. Use the tools we have — Grove for policies, Fern for people data, Ledgerly for expenses, 1Password for credentials — and remember that not everyone reads every channel every day.

If a discussion in a channel goes back and forth more than a few times, that is a signal to move it to a short synchronous conversation. Async does not mean slow; it means thoughtful. When a decision requires rapid iteration or high emotional nuance, a meeting is the right tool.

Sofia, a product manager in Fleet Insights working remotely from Spain, drafts a product decision document and shares it with her team a few days before the decision deadline. Colleagues comment asynchronously, and Sofia incorporates their feedback. By the time the team meets, the discussion is short and the decision is almost made. The meeting exists to confirm, not to discover.

## 5. Questions

We encourage you to question the meetings you are invited to. If you believe your presence is not needed, decline the invitation with a polite note — this is not rudeness, it is respect for everyone's time. If you believe a meeting lacks purpose or an agenda, say so to the host before the meeting, not during it.

We also encourage you to question meetings you host. After a meeting, ask yourself: did we need it? Did we achieve what we set out to do? Could it have been an email, a document, or a recording? If the answer leans toward "no" or "yes, it could have been async," change the approach next time.

If you feel that a recurring meeting has outlived its usefulness, raise it. Propose cancelling it, reducing its frequency, or changing its format. The person who called the meeting into question is doing the whole company a favour.

For questions about this guideline, or for help designing a better meeting practice for your team, reach out to your manager or the Leadership Team. For practical support with scheduling tools or calendar settings, contact it-help@kranich.example. For anything related to how we work together as people, people@kranich.example is always open.

Example: Marek, a field solutions engineer working with clients in Poland, receives an invitation to a weekly internal status call. He checks the agenda and realises the update is already covered in the shared project tracker. He declines politely, noting that he will flag anything urgent directly. The host thanks him — one fewer attendee makes the call shorter for everyone else.

We trust you to use this guideline with good sense. Meetings are not the enemy; thoughtless meetings are. Protect your focus, respect your colleagues' time, and keep the conversation going — async first, synchronous when it matters.
