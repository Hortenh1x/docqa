# Managing Remote Teams

## 1. Purpose

Kranich Software GmbH is a remote-first company. Most of us work from home within our country of employment, and we have offices in Berlin and Lisbon for those who prefer a desk or a meeting room. Managing a team that is spread across countries and time zones is a skill, not a default. This document explains how we expect people managers to lead, support, and grow their teams in this remote-first setting.

We believe that good remote management is simply good management, with more intention. You cannot rely on overhearing a conversation or catching someone in the kitchen. You have to create the conditions for clarity, connection, and trust on purpose.

## 2. Who this is for

This document is for everyone at Kranich who leads people, formally or informally. That includes engineering managers, team leads, and anyone who mentors a colleague. It is also useful for individual contributors who want to understand what they can expect from their manager.

If you are new to managing a remote team, read this document together with the relevant policies in the Grove policy library. Your People & Culture partner can also walk you through the expectations described here.

## 3. Process

### 3.1 Set clear expectations early

Remote work fails when expectations are vague. At the start of any working relationship, agree with each team member on:

- Working hours and overlap with the rest of the team.
- How and when they prefer to be reached.
- What "done" looks like for their role.
- How they want to receive feedback.

Write these agreements down and revisit them when circumstances change. For example, if a team member moves or changes their schedule, update the agreement together.

### 3.2 Communicate with intention

Do not assume silence means everything is fine. In a remote setting, you have to check in deliberately. Use the internal tools we have: Perch for booking time together, and Grove for sharing notes and decisions.

Prefer asynchronous communication by default. Write clear, concise messages that a colleague in another time zone can act on without waiting for you. When a topic is complex or sensitive, switch to a video call.

### 3.3 Run meetings that respect attention

Meetings are a scarce resource in a remote company. Before scheduling one, ask whether the goal can be achieved asynchronously. When you do meet:

- Share an agenda beforehand.
- Start and end on time.
- Give everyone a chance to speak, especially quieter team members.
- Capture decisions and action items during the call.

Example: Deniz, a customer success manager in Berlin, leads a team with colleagues in Poland and Spain. She schedules the weekly team call during the overlap in the middle of the day, shares the agenda the day before, and rotates who facilitates the discussion.

### 3.4 Support wellbeing and boundaries

Remote work can blur the line between home and work. Watch for signs that a team member is overworking or feels isolated. Encourage them to take breaks and to disconnect at the end of their day.

Respect their documented working hours. If you send a message outside those hours, make clear that a response is not expected until their next working day. Never praise someone primarily for how many hours they put in; praise outcomes and quality.

### 3.5 Give feedback that helps people grow

Feedback should be regular, specific, and kind. Do not wait for a formal review cycle to tell someone they are doing well or to raise a concern. In a remote setting, small issues can grow quietly if left unaddressed.

Use the "situation, behavior, impact" format. Describe the situation, the behavior you observed, and the impact it had. Then invite a conversation.

Example: Marek, a field solutions engineer working with clients in Poland, recently missed a client follow-up. His manager did not assume intent. Instead, she asked what had happened, learned that Marek had not seen the message because it was sent to a channel he rarely checks, and together they agreed on a better way to reach him.

### 3.6 Develop people across distances

Career growth should not depend on being visible in the office. Have regular one-on-one conversations that are about the person, not just the project. Ask about their goals, what they enjoy, and what they find difficult.

Look for opportunities to stretch your team members: a presentation, a cross-team collaboration, or a chance to mentor someone else. Make sure these opportunities are distributed fairly and not only to people who happen to be in the same city as you.

### 3.7 Handle performance concerns directly

If a team member is not meeting expectations, address it promptly and directly. Remote management can make it tempting to avoid difficult conversations, but that helps no one.

Describe the specific gap you see, ask for their perspective, and agree on a plan to improve. Involve your People & Culture partner early so that the process is fair and consistent with our policies. See §3 of POL-004 for guidance on performance improvement plans.

### 3.8 Build team culture on purpose

Culture does not happen by accident in a remote company. Spend time building shared rituals that work for your team. This might be a short weekly check-in, a virtual coffee, or a shared channel for non-work chat.

Be mindful that not everyone enjoys the same activities. Offer a range of ways to connect and never make participation feel mandatory.

Example: Anna, a backend engineer in Lisbon, is part of a team where most colleagues are in Berlin. Her manager rotates meeting times so that Anna is not always the one joining early or late, and he checks in with her separately to make sure she feels included in decisions.

## 4. Timelines

Remote management is not a one-time project. It is a continuous practice. However, some moments deserve extra attention:

- When a new team member joins, invest time in their first weeks to set expectations and build relationships.
- When someone changes roles or teams, revisit the agreements you made together.
- When a team member changes their location or working pattern, update their working agreement and inform the wider team.
- At the start of each calendar year, review your team's ways of working and adjust what is not serving them.

There is no fixed schedule for these reviews. Use your judgment and check in with your team about what they need.
