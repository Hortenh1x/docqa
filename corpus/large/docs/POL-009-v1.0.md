# Code of Conduct

## 1. Purpose

This Code of Conduct sets out the standards we hold ourselves to at Kranich Software GmbH. It is a practical guide for everyday decisions, not a legal document. It applies to everyone who works with us — whether you are employed in Berlin, Lisbon, or remotely from another country, whether you are in a permanent role or supporting us as a contractor.

This version supersedes version 0.9. If you have been relying on an older copy, please familiarise yourself with the current one. The Code sits alongside our other policies, including POL-004 on information security. Where another policy goes into more detail on a specific topic, that policy governs.

We build route-optimization software for logistics operators. Our customers trust us with their operations, and our colleagues trust us with their working relationships. That trust is earned through consistent, honest behaviour. This Code helps us protect it.

## 2. How we treat each other

We are a remote-first company with people in several countries, and we collaborate across time zones and cultures. The way we treat each other is the foundation of everything else.

Treat every colleague with respect, regardless of their role, team, location, or background. Listen before you respond. Assume good intent, and give others the benefit of the doubt when something is ambiguous. When you disagree, disagree with the idea, not the person.

We do not tolerate harassment, discrimination, or bullying in any form. This applies to all interactions: in meetings, in chat channels, in email, and at company events. If you see behaviour that crosses a line, you have a responsibility to speak up — either to the person directly, to your manager, or through the channels described in §5.

Be inclusive in how you work. Involve colleagues who are in different time zones. Write down important decisions so that people who could not attend a meeting can catch up. Give credit where it is due, and acknowledge contributions from others openly.

We also treat each other's time with care. Start meetings on time, end them on time, and only invite people who truly need to be there. If you cannot attend, let the organiser know promptly rather than leaving them wondering.

Example: Deniz, a customer success manager in Berlin, notices that a colleague in Lisbon has been excluded from a discussion about a client handover. She adds the colleague to the thread and summarises the key points so they can contribute fully. This is the kind of small, deliberate action that keeps our remote culture healthy.

## 3. Gifts and hospitality

Building relationships with clients, partners, and vendors is part of our work. Exchanging small tokens of appreciation is normal in business. However, gifts and hospitality can create the appearance of impropriety, even when none exists.

You may accept or offer ordinary business hospitality, such as a working lunch or a modest meal, as long as it is reasonable, transparent, and does not influence a business decision. Gifts and hospitality above €60 in value must be declared. This applies both to gifts you receive and gifts you give on behalf of Kranich. You can make the declaration in Grove, where the form will guide you through the details.

Do not accept cash or cash equivalents, regardless of the amount. Do not accept gifts that could reasonably be seen as an attempt to influence a decision you are involved in. If you are unsure whether something is appropriate, err on the side of transparency and declare it, or ask your manager.

When you offer gifts to clients or partners, make sure they comply with the recipient's own policies, where those are known to you. A gift that is unremarkable for us might be problematic for a public-sector client, for example. Use good judgement.

Example: Marek, a field solutions engineer working with clients in Poland, is offered a weekend stay at a supplier's holiday apartment after a successful pilot. The value is well above €60. Marek politely declines and explains that he cannot accept hospitality of that kind under our Code of Conduct. He suggests a working lunch instead, which he can accept within the rules.

## 4. Conflicts of interest

A conflict of interest arises when your personal interests could influence, or appear to influence, your decisions on behalf of Kranich. Conflicts are not inherently wrong — they become a problem when they are hidden or when they affect your judgement.

Disclose any situation that could create a conflict. This includes: close personal relationships with colleagues in your reporting line, significant financial interests in a supplier, customer, or competitor, and outside employment or business activities that compete with Kranich. Disclosure is not an admission of wrongdoing; it is a way of protecting you and the company.

If you are involved in hiring, procurement, or any decision where a personal relationship exists, step back from the decision and let someone independent handle it. If you are unsure whether something counts as a conflict, describe the situation to your manager or to People & Culture and let them help you assess it.

We expect full honesty in expense reporting, time tracking, and all other administrative processes. Claiming reimbursement for something that was not a genuine business expense is a form of conflict between your interests and the company's.

Example: Sofia, a product manager in Fleet Insights, learns that her partner's company is bidding to provide a data service to Kranich. She does not participate in the evaluation and informs her manager before the process begins. Her manager appoints an independent colleague to lead the review.

## 5. Speaking up

We want issues to surface early, when they are easiest to fix. If you see something that concerns you — a potential violation of this Code, a safety issue, or behaviour that does not feel right — please raise it.

There are several ways to speak up. You can talk to your manager, to anyone in the Leadership Team, or to People & Culture. You can also write to people@kranich.example. If your concern relates to security or data protection, you can contact security@kranich.example instead. Choose whichever channel you are most comfortable with.

We do not tolerate retaliation against anyone who raises a concern in good faith, regardless of where the concern ultimately leads. Making a mistake and admitting it is always better than hiding it. If you are unsure whether something is worth raising, raise it anyway — a brief conversation is a small price for clarity.

When you raise a concern, we will take it seriously and handle it as confidentially as the circumstances allow. In some cases, we may need to involve others to investigate properly, but we will not share your name unnecessarily.

Example: Anna, a backend engineer in Lisbon, notices that a colleague in another team has been approving expenses for a vendor they have a personal stake in. She is uncomfortable approaching the colleague directly, so she writes to people@kranich.example describing what she saw. People & Culture reviews the situation without revealing Anna's identity to the colleague involved.

## 6. Questions

This Code cannot cover every situation you will encounter. When in doubt, ask. A quick question is almost always cheaper than untangling a problem later.

For questions about how this Code applies to a specific situation, start with your manager. For questions about gifts, conflicts of interest, or speaking up, you can also reach out to People & Culture at people@kranich.example. For anything related to information security or data handling, contact security@kranich.example.

If you ask a question, you will receive a straight answer. If we do not know, we will find out and come back to you. There is no such thing as a silly question when it comes to ethics and integrity.

We review this Code periodically to make sure it stays relevant to how we work. If you have suggestions for improvement, please share them with the Leadership Team or through People & Culture. Your perspective helps us make Kranich a better place to work for everyone.
