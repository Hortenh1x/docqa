# Disciplinary Procedure

Access: Managers only

## 1. Purpose

This procedure sets out how we handle concerns about employee conduct or performance that fall short of our expectations. It is designed to be fair, consistent, and respectful — for the employee concerned, for the team, and for Kranich as a whole.

We believe that most issues are best resolved through honest conversation and support. Discipline is a last resort, not a first response. This procedure exists so that when formal action is necessary, it happens in a way that is transparent, documented, and aligned with our values.

The goal is always to help employees improve. Where that is not possible, this procedure provides a clear path to manage the situation fairly and lawfully, in line with the employment legislation of the country where the employee works.

## 2. Who this is for

This document is for managers at Kranich Software GmbH and at our Lisbon office. It applies when you manage an employee in any of the countries where we employ people: Germany, Portugal, Spain, Poland, France, and the United Kingdom.

If you are an employee and have questions about this procedure, please speak to your manager or to People & Culture. This document is confidential to managers, but its principles — fairness, transparency, and support — apply to everyone.

As a manager, you are expected to use this procedure thoughtfully. You are not expected to be a legal expert. You are expected to recognise when a situation has moved beyond a casual conversation and needs a structured response. When in doubt, contact People & Culture before you act.

## 3. Process

### 3.1 Before formal discipline

Before considering formal discipline, have a conversation with the employee. Explain the concern clearly, listen to their perspective, and agree on what needs to change. Offer support where appropriate — for example, additional training, clearer objectives, or a check-in with their engineering manager or team lead.

Most issues are resolved at this stage. If the concern is resolved and does not recur, no further action is needed. If the concern persists or is serious enough that a conversation alone is not appropriate, move to the formal process.

### 3.2 Formal investigation

When formal discipline may be needed, People & Culture will support you in conducting a fair investigation. This means gathering relevant facts, speaking to the employee concerned, and speaking to any witnesses. The employee has the right to be accompanied by a colleague or a union representative where local law provides for this.

Investigations should be proportionate. A minor concern may need only a short conversation with the employee. A serious concern may require a more thorough review. Throughout, treat the matter as confidential and share information only with those who need to know.

### 3.3 Disciplinary meeting

If the investigation suggests that formal action is warranted, arrange a disciplinary meeting. Give the employee reasonable notice in writing. Explain the nature of the concern and provide any relevant documents in advance so they can prepare.

At the meeting, present the concern, allow the employee to respond, and ask questions to clarify their account. The employee may be accompanied, as above. Take notes and summarise the discussion afterwards.

Do not decide the outcome before the meeting. Keep an open mind and consider everything the employee says.

### 3.4 Decision

After the meeting, consider the evidence and the employee's response. Decide whether the concern is upheld, and if so, what action is appropriate. Possible outcomes include:

- No further action, where the concern is not upheld or is minor.
- A formal written warning, where the concern is upheld but the employee can improve with clear expectations.
- A final written warning, where the concern is serious or where a previous warning has not led to improvement.
- Dismissal, in the most serious cases, such as gross misconduct, or where improvement has not been possible despite warnings.

Explain the decision to the employee in writing. Set out the reasons, the action being taken, and the period for which a warning will remain active. Explain that they have the right to appeal.

### 3.5 Appeal

An employee may appeal a disciplinary decision. Appeals should be made in writing within a reasonable period after the decision. A different manager, usually from outside the employee's team, will hear the appeal. The appeal may consider new evidence, procedural errors, or whether the decision was reasonable. The outcome of the appeal may uphold, overturn, or vary the original decision.

Example: Deniz, a customer success manager in Berlin, received a formal written warning after a complaint from a client. Deniz felt that the investigation had missed key context and appealed. A manager from the Platform & Infrastructure team reviewed the case, spoke to Deniz and the client, and revised the warning to no further action after finding that the complaint was based on a misunderstanding.

### 3.6 Records

People & Culture will keep a confidential record of any formal disciplinary process, including the investigation, the meeting, the decision, and the appeal. Access to these records is restricted to People & Culture and, where necessary, the employee's manager. Records are kept in line with our data protection obligations and are deleted after the relevant retention period.

## 4. Timelines

We aim to handle disciplinary matters promptly. Delays are stressful for everyone involved, so we move as quickly as fairness allows.

- An investigation should begin as soon as reasonably possible after the concern comes to light.
- The employee should receive notice of a disciplinary meeting well in advance, giving them time to prepare.
- A decision should be communicated soon after the meeting, once the evidence has been properly considered.
- An appeal should be heard promptly after it is received.

If a process takes longer than expected — for example, because witnesses are on leave or because the matter is complex — keep the employee informed of progress.

Example: Marek, a field solutions engineer working with clients in Poland, was involved as a witness in a disciplinary matter concerning a colleague. The investigation took longer than usual because Marek was travelling for client work. His manager kept him updated and arranged a call at a time that worked across time zones.

## 5. Questions

If you have questions about this procedure, contact People & Culture at people@kranich.example.

If you believe a disciplinary process has been handled unfairly — either for yourself or for someone you manage — raise it with People & Culture or, if appropriate, with the VP People & Culture. You can also refer to related policies in the Grove policy library, including POL-004, which covers expected conduct in more detail.

If the concern relates to a serious breach of security or data protection, contact the Head of Information Security at security@kranich.example before proceeding under this procedure.

Example: Sofia, a product manager in Fleet Insights, was unsure whether a performance concern with a team member warranted formal discipline or a more supportive approach. She spoke to People & Culture, who helped her distinguish between a capability issue and a conduct issue, and advised her on the appropriate path. The matter was resolved through coaching and did not proceed to formal discipline.

Remember that this procedure is a framework, not a script. Every situation is different. Use your judgement, seek advice early, and treat every employee with dignity throughout the process.
