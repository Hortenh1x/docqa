# 1:1 Meetings Guide

## 1. Purpose

Regular 1:1 meetings are one of the most important habits we build at Kranich Software. They give you and your manager dedicated, uninterrupted time to step back from daily tasks and talk about what matters: your work, your growth, and how you are feeling about both.

A good 1:1 is not a status update. Status updates belong in project channels or async updates. A 1:1 is a space for conversation, feedback, and support — for you to raise what is on your mind and for your manager to help you navigate challenges and opportunities.

This guide describes how we run 1:1s across the company, so that everyone has a consistent, useful experience regardless of team or location.

## 2. Who this is for

This guide applies to every employee at Kranich Software, in all teams and all locations — whether you work from the Ritterstraße office in Berlin, the Rua do Alecrim office in Lisbon, or remotely from home in Germany, Portugal, Spain, Poland, France, or the United Kingdom.

It is written primarily for two audiences:

- **Employees** who have a manager and want to make the most of their 1:1 time.
- **Managers** who run 1:1s and want a clear, shared framework.

If you are an engineering manager, a team lead, or anyone else who manages people, this guide is your reference. If you are a people manager of managers, you should also hold 1:1s with your direct reports and use the same principles.

Example: Deniz, a customer success manager in Berlin, meets with her manager every few weeks. She uses the time to talk about a difficult client relationship and to ask for coaching on how to handle the next conversation. Her manager listens, asks questions, and shares a relevant approach from another team. Deniz leaves with a clearer plan.

## 3. Process

### Scheduling and cadence

Your manager is responsible for scheduling regular 1:1s with you. If you do not have a recurring meeting on your calendar, ask your manager to set one up, or reach out to People & Culture for help.

The cadence should be regular and predictable. Most teams meet every week or every other week. Choose a rhythm that works for both of you and stick to it. Reschedule rather than cancel whenever possible — a 1:1 that is skipped repeatedly sends a signal that the conversation is not important.

### Agenda

You own the agenda. This is your time, and you should bring what you want to discuss. A few categories to consider:

- **Current work**: what is going well, what is blocked, where you need input.
- **Feedback**: both giving and receiving. Praise, concerns, and suggestions for improvement.
- **Growth and development**: skills you want to build, roles you are curious about, long-term aspirations.
- **Well-being and workload**: how you are doing, whether the pace is sustainable, and what support you need.
- **Team and company**: observations, ideas, and questions about how things work around you.

Your manager may also add items to the agenda — for example, to share updates, give feedback, or align on priorities. Both sides should prepare a little before the meeting so the time is used well.

Example: Anna, a backend engineer in Lisbon, keeps a running list of topics in her notes app. Before her 1:1, she adds two items: a question about how the Routing Core team plans its technical debt work, and a request for feedback on the code review she submitted last week. When the meeting starts, she shares her screen and walks through her list.

### During the meeting

Start by checking in on how the other person is doing — genuinely. Then move through the agenda. As a manager, listen more than you speak. Ask open questions. Resist the urge to jump into problem-solving mode immediately; often the employee has a solution in mind and just needs space to articulate it.

Take notes during the meeting, but keep your attention on the conversation. After the meeting, write down any action items and who owns them.

### After the meeting

Follow up on what you agreed. If you said you would do something, do it promptly. If you raised a concern, check back on it in the next 1:1. This follow-through builds trust and shows that the meeting matters.

## 4. Timelines

1:1s should happen on a regular, ongoing basis throughout the year. There is no fixed schedule that applies to everyone — the right frequency depends on your role, your experience, and the nature of your work.

As a general expectation:

- **New employees** should meet with their manager more frequently during their first weeks to support onboarding and early alignment.
- **People in new or changing roles** may benefit from a temporary increase in frequency until they feel settled.
- **Experienced employees in stable roles** may prefer a lighter cadence, but should still meet regularly enough that conversations feel continuous rather than occasional.

If you feel that your 1:1s are too rare or too frequent, say so. Your manager can adjust. If you and your manager disagree about the right cadence, escalate to your manager's manager or to People & Culture.

Additionally, at least once per year, every employee should have a dedicated conversation about their development and performance that goes beyond the regular 1:1. This conversation is separate from day-to-day check-ins and is described in the relevant People & Culture policy (see POL-004). Your manager will schedule this conversation for you.

Example: Marek, a field solutions engineer who works with clients in Poland, recently moved into a new account territory. His manager proposes meeting every week for the first few weeks to help him get oriented, then returning to the usual rhythm. Marek agrees, and the extra check-ins help him clarify expectations with two new clients early on.

## 5. Questions

A good 1:1 is driven by good questions. Here are some prompts you can use, whether you are the employee or the manager.

**For employees to ask themselves before the meeting:**

- What do I want to get out of this conversation?
- What is taking more energy than it should?
- Where do I need help or input?
- What feedback do I want to give or receive?

**For employees to ask their manager:**

- What is going well in my work that I should keep doing?
- What should I stop, start, or change?
- How can I better support the team's goals?
- What opportunities for growth do you see for me?

**For managers to ask their direct reports:**

- How are you doing, really?
- What is the most useful thing we can talk about today?
- What is blocking you or slowing you down?
- Is there anything you need from me that you are not getting?
- What feedback do you have for me or for the team?

**For both to consider:**

- What are we not talking about that we should be?
- What has changed since our last conversation?
- What would make our 1:1s more useful?

Example: Sofia, a product manager for Fleet Insights working remotely from Spain, uses the last few minutes of her 1:1 to ask her manager a direct question: "What is one thing I could do differently that would make the biggest difference?" Her manager pauses and shares a suggestion about how Sofia communicates trade-offs to the engineering team. Sofia takes it as constructive and tries a new approach in the next planning session.

Remember: the best 1:1s are honest, curious, and forward-looking. They are a habit that pays off over time. If you have questions about how to run or prepare for a 1:1, reach out to your manager or to People & Culture at people@kranich.example.
