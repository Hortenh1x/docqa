# Customer Onboarding Guide

## 1. Scope

This guide describes how we welcome new customers to Kranich Route Cloud and accompany them through their first months with our software. It is written for Customer Success Managers, field solutions engineers, and anyone else who touches a customer during onboarding.

Our goal is simple: help each customer become confident and self-sufficient with route optimization as quickly as possible. We measure success by whether the customer uses the product regularly and can explain its value to their own stakeholders.

The guide covers the three phases of onboarding: kick-off, data import, and go-live. It complements the internal playbooks referenced in POL-004 and the commercial terms described in FIN-RATES-2026.

## 2. Kick-off

Onboarding begins with a kick-off meeting. The Customer Success Manager leads it, and the field solutions engineer joins when the customer operates in a country where we have deployed field staff.

The kick-off has three purposes. First, we get to know the customer's operations: how many vehicles they run, what kinds of stops they serve, and which constraints matter most to them — such as time windows, driver breaks, or vehicle capacities. Second, we align on roles: who on the customer side will be the daily user, who approves changes, and who we should contact if something needs escalation. Third, we set expectations about what the onboarding journey looks like and what we need from the customer along the way.

We recommend that the customer designates a single point of contact. This person does not need to be technical, but they should have the authority to gather information from their own operations team.

Example: Deniz, a Customer Success Manager in Berlin, starts every kick-off by asking the customer to describe a typical day in their depot. This helps her understand their reality before she talks about features.

After the kick-off, we send a short summary to the customer, confirming the agreed roles and the next steps. We also schedule regular check-ins for the remainder of the onboarding, roughly weekly at first and less frequently as the customer becomes more confident.

## 3. Data import

Before the customer can plan routes, they need their data in Kranich Route Cloud. This phase is often the most demanding, because data quality varies a great deal between customers.

We import three main types of data: stops or delivery points, vehicles, and driver information. Stops typically come from the customer's order management system. Vehicles and drivers often live in spreadsheets or in a fleet management tool. We do not require a particular format; we accept common spreadsheet exports and structured data files. What matters is that the data is clean and complete.

We provide a data checklist ahead of time. It covers the fields we need, such as addresses, opening hours, vehicle capacities, and driver working times. We also ask the customer to flag any stops that are seasonal or temporary, so we can decide together how to handle them.

During the import, our team validates the data and flags issues for the customer to fix. Common issues include duplicate addresses, missing postal codes, and vehicles with zero capacity. We do not silently correct data; the customer must confirm every change we propose, because their data is their truth.

Example: Marek, a field solutions engineer working with clients in Poland, often visits customers in person during this phase. He brings a laptop, sits with their dispatchers, and walks through the data line by line. This builds trust and prevents misunderstandings later.

Once the data is clean, we run a test optimization. This gives the customer a first look at what Kranich Route Cloud can do with their real data. We treat this test as a learning exercise, not a final result.

## 4. Go-live

Go-live is the moment when the customer starts using Kranich Route Cloud for their daily planning instead of their previous process. We prepare for it carefully, because a smooth go-live protects the customer's operations and our reputation.

Before go-live, we agree on a pilot period. During this time, the customer runs their planning in parallel with their old process, so they can compare results and build confidence. The length of the pilot depends on the customer's complexity; we let the customer set the pace, as long as they remain engaged.

We also prepare the customer's team with short training sessions. These cover the daily workflow: importing orders, generating routes, adjusting plans manually, and exporting routes to the drivers. We keep training practical and scenario-based. We do not walk through every feature; we focus on what the customer will use every day.

On the day of go-live, the Customer Success Manager is available throughout the working hours of the customer's operation. The field solutions engineer joins if the customer is in a country with field coverage. We expect a few teething problems, and we respond promptly when they occur.

After go-live, we continue to check in with the customer at a decreasing frequency. We look at usage patterns and ask the customer directly how things are going. If we see that a feature is not being used, we ask why. Sometimes the answer is that the customer does not need it; sometimes it reveals a gap in training or a bug we need to fix.

Example: Sofia, a Product Manager in Fleet Insights, attends go-live reviews for selected customers. She listens for patterns that could improve the product for everyone, and she feeds those insights back to the engineering teams.

Go-live is not the end of onboarding. We consider onboarding complete when the customer can plan their routes independently, understands the reports we provide, and knows how to reach us when they need help. From that point, the relationship continues under the regular care of Customer Success, guided by the principles in POL-004.
