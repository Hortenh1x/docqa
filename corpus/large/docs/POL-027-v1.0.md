# Security Incident Policy

## 1. Purpose

At Kranich Software GmbH, we build software that helps logistics operators keep their fleets moving. Our customers trust us with sensitive data about their routes, vehicles, and business operations. Protecting that data is a shared responsibility across the whole company.

This policy explains what a security incident is, how to report one, and what happens after you raise the alarm. It is written for everyone at Kranich — whether you work from the Ritterstraße office in Berlin, the Rua do Alecrim office in Lisbon, or remotely from your home in Germany, Portugal, Spain, Poland, France, or the United Kingdom.

We want you to feel confident that reporting a suspected problem is always the right thing to do. You will never be blamed for raising a concern in good faith, even if it turns out to be a false alarm. Acting quickly and transparently protects our customers, our colleagues, and our reputation.

## 2. What counts as an incident

A security incident is any event that could compromise the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of Kranich data or systems. It does not need to be confirmed or fully understood before you report it. If something feels off, treat it as a suspected incident and follow the steps in §3 of this policy.

Examples of incidents include:

- A laptop or mobile device is lost or stolen, especially if it was unlocked or contains customer data.
- You receive a phishing email that looks like it comes from a Kranich service, or you accidentally click a link in a suspicious message.
- You notice unusual activity in a Kranich application, such as accounts you do not recognise, unexpected permission changes, or data that appears altered.
- Customer data is sent to the wrong person, or you see data in a place where it should not be.
- A system, service, or backup appears to be unavailable for reasons you cannot explain.
- You lose a physical item that contains credentials, notes, or access information, such as a notebook with passwords written down.

Example: Marek, a field solutions engineer working with clients in Poland, notices that a customer's route history looks different from what the customer described. He is not sure whether this is a bug or something more serious. Under this policy, he reports his observation promptly rather than waiting for proof — the investigation can determine what happened.

Not every technical problem is a security incident. A routine software bug that does not involve data exposure or unauthorised access is usually handled through the normal product process. If you are unsure which category applies, report it as a suspected incident anyway. The Information Security team would rather review a concern that turns out to be minor than miss one that matters.

## 3. Reporting

If you suspect a security incident, report it within 24 hours to security@kranich.example. The 24 hours start from the moment you become aware of the suspected issue, not from the moment you confirm it.

When you write your report, include as much as you can about:

- What happened and when you first noticed it.
- Which systems, devices, or data are involved.
- Any steps you have already taken in response.
- Your contact details, so the team can follow up with you.

You do not need to investigate the issue yourself or gather evidence before reporting. Your job is to raise the concern; the Information Security team will take it from there.

If the incident involves a physical item, such as a lost laptop or a stolen access badge, also inform your manager and, if you are in an office, the Head of Workplace & Operations. For anything else, the email alias is sufficient.

Example: Deniz, a customer success manager in Berlin, receives an email that appears to come from a customer but contains a link to a login page that looks wrong. She does not click the link. Instead, she forwards the email to security@kranich.example within 24 hours and notes that she has not opened the attachment.

After you submit a report, you will receive an acknowledgement that the message was received. The Information Security team will assign someone to assess the situation and will keep you informed of next steps.

## 4. Containment

Once an incident is reported, the Information Security team takes the lead on containing it. The goal of containment is to stop the incident from spreading and to prevent further harm while the team investigates.

Containment steps may include:

- Disconnecting an affected device from the network.
- Revoking access credentials or sessions.
- Isolating a service or temporarily taking it offline.
- Restoring data from a known-good backup.
- Asking you to stop using a particular system until the team understands what happened.

You should not attempt containment measures on your own unless the Information Security team explicitly asks you to do so. Acting without guidance can destroy evidence or make the situation worse. If you have already taken a reasonable step before reporting, such as locking your screen or closing a suspicious email, tell the team what you did.

During containment, the team may ask you questions about your activities leading up to the incident. Answer honestly and completely. There is no blame in this process — the focus is on understanding what happened so the team can respond effectively.

Example: Anna, a backend engineer in Lisbon, notices that a service account in the Routing Core environment has permissions it should not have. She reports it to security@kranich.example within 24 hours. The Information Security team responds by revoking the account's access while they investigate how the permissions were changed. Anna does not modify the account herself; she waits for guidance from the team.

Containment is not the same as resolution. An incident may remain in a contained state while the team investigates the root cause. You will be told when the incident is considered closed and normal operations can resume.

## 5. Disclosure decisions

Decisions about external disclosure are made by the Information Security team together with the leadership team, including the CEO and the Head of Information Security. These decisions consider our legal obligations, our contractual commitments to customers, and the risk to affected parties.

You should never disclose information about an ongoing incident to external parties — including customers, partners, journalists, or on social media — unless you are explicitly authorised to do so. This includes discussing the incident with people outside Kranich, even if they are trusted contacts.

If a customer asks you about a suspected incident, do not confirm or deny anything. Instead, tell them that you have noted their question and that the appropriate team will follow up. Then report the inquiry to the Information Security team so they are aware of external interest.

Internal communication about incidents is handled through the appropriate channels. The Information Security team will share updates with affected teams as needed. If you are not directly involved in the response, you may not receive details about an ongoing incident — this is normal and helps keep the response focused.

Example: Sofia, a product manager in the Fleet Insights team, receives a call from a customer who heard about a service disruption and asks whether customer data was exposed. Sofia explains that she cannot comment on operational matters and that the customer will receive a response from the appropriate team. She then reports the call to security@kranich.example so the team can decide how to respond.

When an incident is resolved, the Information Security team may issue a summary internally so that colleagues can learn from what happened. These summaries focus on lessons learned and improvements, not on assigning blame.

## 6. Questions

If you have any questions about this policy or about whether a particular situation counts as an incident, contact security@kranich.example. You can also reach out to the Head of Information Security directly.

For questions about how this policy relates to your employment or your team's ways of working, contact people@kranich.example or speak with your manager. The People & Culture team can help you understand your responsibilities and point you to related guidance.

Example: Lea, a people operations specialist in Berlin, is unsure whether an employee's report about a suspicious message in the Fern HRIS should go through this policy or through a different channel. She sends a question to security@kranich.example, and the team confirms that any suspected issue with a Kranich system, including internal tools, should be reported under this policy.

Remember: when in doubt, report. A prompt report gives the Information Security team the best chance to protect Kranich, our customers, and each other.
